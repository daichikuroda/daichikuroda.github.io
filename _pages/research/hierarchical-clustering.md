## Clustering is everywhere

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/clustering-at-work.svg" alt="Six small illustrations: a phylogenetic tree with colored clades, cells grouped into cell types, a network split into communities, a nearest-neighbor search that visits only the clusters closest to a query, images labelled by their cluster for self-supervised learning, and documents organized into a tree of clusters and summaries." caption="Clustering at work: on its own (top) and inside machine learning systems (bottom)." %}

Clustering is one of the most widely used tasks in machine learning and data analysis. On its own, it helps make sense of data, from grouping species into clades and cells into cell types to finding communities in networks. It is also a building block of larger machine learning systems: it speeds up nearest-neighbor search (Jégou et al., 2011; Johnson et al., 2019), provides pseudo-labels for self-supervised learning (Caron et al., 2018, 2020), and organizes documents for retrieval by language models (Sarthi et al., 2024).

## …but ill-posed

The task is to group similar points into the same group, a *cluster*, while putting dissimilar points into different clusters. However, clustering is an ill-posed problem. There is no consensus on what a cluster is, and the same data often admit several equally sensible clusterings (Figure 1). This is more than a practical nuisance: Kleinberg (2002) proved that no clustering method can satisfy three natural requirements at the same time ([details below](#kleinbergs-impossibility-theorem)).

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/flat-clusterings.svg" alt="The same 25 points shown three times: unlabeled, split into two clusters, and split into five clusters." caption="**Figure 1.** Which clustering is correct? The data in (a) can be split into two clusters (b) or into five (c). Both answers are sensible: they describe the data at different resolutions." %}

## Hierarchical clustering

Hierarchical clustering generalizes clustering: instead of a single partition, it recovers *nested* clusters, organized as a tree (Figure 2). It has well-known advantages over flat clustering. It describes the cluster structure at several resolutions at once, and it does not need to know the number of clusters in advance, unlike standard algorithms such as $$k$$-means.

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/hierarchy-example.svg" alt="The points of Figure 1 with nested clusters A and B, and the corresponding tree in which A splits into two sub-clusters and B into three." caption="**Figure 2.** A hierarchical clustering of the data of Figure 1. (a) Nested clusters: A and B, each made of smaller clusters. (b) The corresponding tree contains both answers of Figure 1, the two clusters and the five clusters. The three sub-clusters of B merge at the same level, so the tree need not be binary." %}

## Our results in brief

Beyond these known advantages, my collaborators and I show that hierarchical clustering is a **well-posed** problem ([Kuroda, Dreveton, Grossglauser & Thiran, 2026](#kuroda2026hierarchical)): hierarchical versions of Kleinberg's three requirements, which no flat clustering method can satisfy together, can all be satisfied by hierarchical clustering methods. We call these methods *admissible*, and there are uncountably many of them.

Furthermore, there is no single best way to cluster hierarchically. Some admissible methods find more clusters than others, but no method finds all the clusters that the others find. Instead, there are infinitely many "best" methods, and on some data they find clusters that cannot belong to the same tree. These methods are therefore genuinely different, which is useful in practice: it leaves room to choose the method that best suits the task at hand. Yet they do not disagree arbitrarily. Every admissible method contains a common backbone of well-separated clusters, and admissible methods differ only in how much additional structure they reveal beyond it. Figure 3 illustrates this.

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/backbone.svg" alt="Four panels: data with two well-separated groups A and B, the backbone tree, and two admissible trees that group the middle sub-group of B differently." caption="**Figure 3.** Schematic illustration. (a) Two well-separated groups, A and B. Inside A, the sub-groups A1 and A2 are clearly separated; inside B, the middle sub-group B2 is about as close to B1 as to B3. (b) The backbone: the clusters that are well separated from the rest of the data (each leaf is one small group of points). Admissible methods contain them once the separation is large enough. (c, d) Admissible methods may reveal further clusters, and they need not agree on them: here one groups B2 with B1, the other B2 with B3. No hierarchy can contain both answers. Figure 7 shows a concrete instance from the paper." %}

## Kleinberg's impossibility theorem

Kleinberg (2002) took an axiomatic approach: rather than proposing yet another algorithm, he asked which properties any clustering method should have. Consider a finite set $$X$$ and a *dissimilarity* $$d$$ on $$X$$, that is, $$d(x, x) = 0$$ and $$d(x, y) = d(y, x) > 0$$ for distinct $$x, y$$ (the triangle inequality is not required). A *flat clustering method* $$f$$ maps every dissimilarity $$d$$ to a partition $$f(d)$$ of $$X$$.

The key notion is a transformation that makes a given clustering more pronounced.

> **Definition** (Strengthening). Let $$\mathcal{C}$$ be a partition of $$X$$. A dissimilarity $$d'$$ is a $$\mathcal{C}$$-*strengthening* of $$d$$ if, for every cluster $$C \in \mathcal{C}$$, it brings points of $$C$$ closer, $$d'(x, y) \le d(x, y)$$ for all $$x, y \in C$$, and pushes them away from the rest, $$d'(x, z) \ge d(x, z)$$ for all $$x \in C$$ and $$z \notin C$$.
{: .definition}

> **Definition** (Kleinberg's axioms). A flat clustering method $$f$$ is
>
> - _scale invariant_ if $$f(\beta d) = f(d)$$ for every $$d$$ and every $$\beta > 0$$: the unit of measurement does not matter;
> - _rich_ if every partition of $$X$$ equals $$f(d)$$ for some $$d$$: no clustering is ruled out in advance;
> - _consistent_ if $$f(d') = f(d)$$ for every $$d$$ and every $$f(d)$$-strengthening $$d'$$ of $$d$$: making the clusters more pronounced does not change the answer.
{: .definition}

> **Theorem** (Kleinberg, 2002). No flat clustering method is simultaneously scale invariant, rich, and consistent.
{: .theorem}

Any two of the three requirements can be satisfied together. Kleinberg shows this with single linkage, which repeatedly merges the two closest clusters: depending on when it stops, it keeps two of the requirements and breaks the third. As we will see, not stopping at all, and keeping the whole tree, breaks none.

## The hierarchical relaxation

Consistency is the most debated of Kleinberg's axioms, and it is also where hierarchies help. A strengthening of a cluster $$C_1 \cup C_2$$ may shrink the distances inside $$C_1$$ and inside $$C_2$$ much more than between them, so that $$C_1$$ and $$C_2$$ become clusters of their own. A consistent flat method is forced to ignore them. A hierarchy can keep $$C_1 \cup C_2$$ and add $$C_1$$ and $$C_2$$ below it, because nested clusters are allowed. More generally, a hierarchy does not need to commit to a single resolution: in Figure 2, the two-cluster and the five-cluster answers of Figure 1 sit in the same tree.

> **Definition** (Hierarchy). A _hierarchy_ on $$X$$ is a collection $$\Psi$$ of subsets of $$X$$, called clusters, such that any two clusters are either nested or disjoint (_laminarity_), and that contains the root $$X$$ and every leaf $$\{x\}$$.
{: .definition}

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/hierarchy.svg" alt="Three panels: seven points with nested clusters; the same hierarchy drawn as a tree with the whole set at the root and single points as leaves; and two overlapping clusters that cannot belong to the same hierarchy." caption="**Figure 4.** (a) A hierarchy on seven points: {a, b} inside {a, b, c}, {f, g} inside {d, e, f, g}, and everything inside the root. (b) The same hierarchy drawn as a tree. (c) Two clusters that overlap without one containing the other cannot both belong to a hierarchy." %}

A *hierarchical clustering method* $$T$$ maps every dissimilarity $$d$$ on $$X$$ to a hierarchy $$T(d)$$ on $$X$$. Kleinberg's axioms have natural analogues, in which a partition is required to *appear in* the hierarchy rather than to *be* the output.

> **Definition** (Admissibility). A hierarchical clustering method $$T$$ is
>
> - _scale invariant_ if $$T(\beta d) = T(d)$$ for every $$d$$ and every $$\beta > 0$$;
> - _partition-rich_ if for every partition $$\mathcal{C}$$ of $$X$$ there is a dissimilarity $$d$$ such that $$\mathcal{C} \subseteq T(d)$$;
> - _partition-consistent_ if, whenever a partition $$\mathcal{C}$$ satisfies $$\mathcal{C} \subseteq T(d)$$, it also satisfies $$\mathcal{C} \subseteq T(d')$$ for every $$\mathcal{C}$$-strengthening $$d'$$ of $$d$$;
> - _permutation invariant_ if relabeling the points relabels the output in the same way.
>
> $$T$$ is _admissible_ if it satisfies all four axioms.
{: .definition}

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/axioms.svg" alt="Four panels illustrating the axioms: enlarging all distances keeps the same clusters; for any grouping of the points, some data make these groups clusters; making clusters tighter and further apart keeps them while new smaller clusters may appear; renaming the points renames the clusters." caption="**Figure 5.** The four axioms, each shown as data before and after a change, with the clusters found by the method outlined. Scale invariance: enlarging all distances does not change the clusters. Partition richness: for any grouping of the points, there are data on which these groups are clusters. Partition consistency: if the points of each cluster are pulled together and different clusters pushed apart, the clusters are kept, and new, smaller clusters may appear inside them. Permutation invariance: renaming the points renames the clusters in the same way." %}

Partition consistency says that strengthening a partition found in the hierarchy keeps it in the hierarchy, while new clusters may appear inside it. Permutation invariance is implicit in Kleinberg's framework and is made explicit here: the output may depend only on the dissimilarities, not on the names of the points. These axioms genuinely extend Kleinberg's: a flat method, viewed as a hierarchy with a single level between the root and the leaves, satisfies the hierarchical versions of scale invariance, richness, and consistency exactly when it satisfies Kleinberg's originals.

> **Theorem** (Kuroda, Dreveton, Grossglauser & Thiran, 2026). There exist uncountably many admissible hierarchical clustering methods.
{: .theorem}

One more natural requirement simplifies the picture. Some dissimilarities are perfectly hierarchical: they are _ultrametrics_, in which, for any three points, the two largest of their three dissimilarities are equal. Every ultrametric comes with a natural hierarchy, in which each dissimilarity is the height at which the two points first end up in the same cluster (Figure 6). We ask that, on such data, a method returns exactly this natural hierarchy. We call such methods _exact on ultrametrics_, and _strongly admissible_ if they are also admissible.

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/ultrametric.svg" alt="A 6 by 6 ultrametric dissimilarity matrix on points a to f and the corresponding tree: a and b merge at height 1, c, d and e merge together at height 2, these five merge at height 3, and all six at height 4." caption="**Figure 6.** An ultrametric and its natural hierarchy. (a) Each entry of the matrix is the height at which the two points first meet in the tree (b): a and b meet at height 1; c, d and e at height 2; these five points at height 3; and all six at height 4. Colors match the levels of the tree." %}

There are also uncountably many strongly admissible methods, and the rest of this page focuses on them. Similar results hold for all admissible methods, with small differences that we point out.

### No single best method

Methods can be compared: $$T_2$$ *refines* $$T_1$$, written $$T_1 \sqsubseteq T_2$$, if $$T_1(d) \subseteq T_2(d)$$ for every $$d$$, so that $$T_2$$ always finds at least the clusters of $$T_1$$, and possibly more. Figure 7 shows three strongly admissible methods ordered in this way.

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/refinement-order.svg" alt="A five-by-five dissimilarity matrix and three hierarchies of the same points, ordered from coarse to fine: global separation finds {1,2}; single linkage adds {1,2,3} and {1,2,3,5}, while stable clusters add {1,2,3} and {1,2,3,4} instead." caption="**Figure 7.** Three strongly admissible methods on the same data, an example from the paper. (a) A dissimilarity on five points. (b) Each arrow points to a finer method, which finds more clusters. At the bottom, global separation finds only {1, 2}. Single linkage adds {1, 2, 3} and {1, 2, 3, 5}, while the stable clusters of Bryant and Berry (2001) add {1, 2, 3} and {1, 2, 3, 4} instead. The clusters {1, 2, 3, 5} and {1, 2, 3, 4} overlap without being nested, so no method can refine both." %}

A greatest strongly admissible method, one that refines all the others, would be the natural "best" method. However, it does not exist. Call two methods *compatible* if, on every input, their outputs can be merged into a single hierarchy.

> **Theorem** (Kuroda, Dreveton, Grossglauser & Thiran, 2026). Strongly admissible methods have no greatest element under refinement. Instead, there are uncountably many maximal strongly admissible methods, and they are mutually incompatible. Every strongly admissible method is refined by at least one of them.
{: .theorem}

The top of Figure 7 shows why: single linkage and stable clusters find clusters that overlap without being nested, so no hierarchy, and hence no method, can contain both outputs. In fact, strongly admissible methods form uncountably long chains of ever finer methods, and uncountably many of them pairwise cannot be combined. The same holds for admissible methods, which also have uncountably many maximal elements that are mutually incompatible.

### A common backbone

Yet strongly admissible methods cannot disagree arbitrarily. Call a set $$C$$ _well separated_ if every dissimilarity inside it is smaller than every dissimilarity between it and the rest of the data:

$$
\max_{x, y \in C} d(x, y) < \min_{x \in C,\, z \notin C} d(x, z).
$$

These are arguably the most conservative clusters: sets of objects that are more similar to each other than to anything else (Figure 8). The well-separated sets of any dissimilarity $$d$$ are nested, so they form a hierarchy $$T_{\mathrm{glob}}(d)$$, and $$T_{\mathrm{glob}}$$, the "global separation" method of Figure 7, is itself strongly admissible.

{% include research-figure.liquid src="/assets/img/research/well-separated.svg" alt="Three panels: a compact set whose largest internal distance is smaller than its distance to the other points; a stretched-out set where a nearby point is closer than the ends of the set are to each other; and a small data set with its nested well-separated sets outlined." caption="**Figure 8.** Well-separated sets. (a) The largest distance inside the set (solid) is smaller than the smallest distance to the other points (dashed): the set is well separated. (b) A stretched-out set: a nearby point is closer to it than its two ends are to each other, so it is not well separated. (c) All the well-separated sets of a small data set: a pair inside a group of three, that group and a second group inside a larger set, and a separate group. They are nested, and form the hierarchy found by global separation." %}

> **Theorem** (Backbone hierarchy; Kuroda, Dreveton, Grossglauser & Thiran, 2026). Every strongly admissible method $$T$$ refines $$T_{\mathrm{glob}}$$: $$T_{\mathrm{glob}}(d) \subseteq T(d)$$ for every dissimilarity $$d$$. Hence $$T_{\mathrm{glob}}$$ is the coarsest strongly admissible method.
{: .theorem}

In other words, every strongly admissible method finds the clusters that are clearly there, and strongly admissible methods differ only in how much additional structure they reveal beyond this common backbone.

A similar picture holds for admissible methods, with one difference: an admissible method need not find every well-separated set, only those that are well separated by a margin. More precisely, for every admissible method there are margins $$0 < \eta_s \le 1$$, one for each cluster size $$s$$, which depend on the method, such that it finds every set $$C$$ with

$$
\max_{x, y \in C} d(x, y) < \eta_{\lvert C \rvert} \cdot \min_{x \in C,\, z \notin C} d(x, z).
$$

Any finite collection of admissible methods therefore shares a common backbone, obtained with the smallest of their margins. Unlike strongly admissible methods, however, admissible methods have no coarsest member: no cluster other than the root and the single points is found by all of them.
